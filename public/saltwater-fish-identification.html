What to Look For

  • Body shape: Fusiform (torpedo) vs. laterally compressed vs. elongated — shape narrows the family immediately.
  • Fin configuration: Number of dorsal fins, presence of spines vs. soft rays, pectoral fin size and position.
  • Lateral line: Visible or hidden? Straight, curved, or absent? This sensory canal's path helps separate families.
  • Mouth position: Terminal (forward-facing), superior (upward), or inferior (downward-facing) indicates feeding habit.
  • Coloration & markings: Note base colour, stripes, spots, and any distinctive patches — but remember that colouration can vary by age, sex, and spawning condition.
  • Scale texture: Ctenoid (rough) vs. cycloid (smooth) scales can be felt or seen under magnification.

Common Saltwater Families & ID Tips

Groupers (Epinephelidae)

Stout body, large mouth, rounded tail. Typically solitary bottom dwellers. Colour varies enormously within species.

Snappers (Lutjanidae)

Pointed snout, forked tail, continuous dorsal fin. Often show a dark spot below the dorsal. Many species form schools.

Jacks (Carangidae)

Streamlined, deeply forked tail, keeled caudal peduncle (scutes). Fast, open-water predators. Silvery with yellow or dark accents.

Mackerel & Tuna (Scombridae)

Spindle-shaped; finlets behind dorsal and anal fins; crescent-shaped tail. Built for sustained high-speed swimming.

Flatfish (Pleuronectidae/Bothidae)

Both eyes on same side; lie flat on the substrate. Identifying which side the eyes are on separates flounders from halibuts.

Wrasses (Labridae)

Elongated body; swim with pectoral fins (labriform locomotion). Hugely diverse — from tiny cleaner wrasse to giant humphead.

Saltwater Regions at a Glance

Geography narrows your search significantly. These broad regions each have distinct endemic and endemic-adjacent species:

  • Indo-Pacific: The richest marine biodiversity on Earth — from East Africa to Polynesia. Home to most parrotfish, butterflyfish, and lionfish.
  • Caribbean / Atlantic: Distinct fauna including tarpon, bonefish, permit, and unique grouper species.
  • Gulf of Mexico: Transition zone with both Atlantic and some Gulf-endemic species including red snapper and spotted seatrout.
  • Eastern Pacific: Garibaldi, sheephead, and various rockfish species not found elsewhere.
  • Mediterranean: Lower diversity but many endemic species; Atlantic species enter via Gibraltar.
