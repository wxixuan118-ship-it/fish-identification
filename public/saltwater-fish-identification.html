Saltwater fish identification by family

When the chart does not give a clean match, place the fish in a family first. Six families account for most saltwater catches:

GroupersEpinephelidae

Stout body, large mouth, rounded tail. Solitary bottom dwellers; colour varies enormously within a species, so use shape and fin count.

SnappersLutjanidae

Pointed snout, forked tail, one continuous dorsal fin. Many show a dark spot below the dorsal and prominent canine teeth.

Jacks, permit and pompanoCarangidae

Streamlined, deeply forked tail, narrow tail wrist often with bony scutes. Fast open-water predators, silver with yellow or dark accents.

Mackerel and tunaScombridae

Spindle-shaped; a row of small finlets behind the dorsal and anal fins; crescent tail. Built for sustained speed.

FlatfishPleuronectidae / Paralichthyidae

Both eyes on one side. Left-eyed = flounders of the Paralichthys group; right-eyed = halibut, plaice and most soles.

Drums and croakersSciaenidae

Redfish, seatrout and croaker. Two dorsal fins that touch; many have a tail-base spot or dark body spots; they audibly "drum".

Saltwater regions at a glance

  • Indo-Pacific: the richest marine fauna on Earth, from East Africa to Polynesia. Home to most parrotfish, butterflyfish and lionfish.
  • Caribbean / W. Atlantic: tarpon, bonefish, permit, snook and Atlantic-only groupers and snappers. See Caribbean fish identification.
  • Gulf of Mexico: red snapper, redfish, spotted seatrout, cobia and king mackerel. See Gulf of Mexico fish identification and Florida fish identification.
  • NW Atlantic (New England to Carolinas): striped bass, bluefish, black sea bass, cod, summer flounder.
  • Eastern Pacific: garibaldi, sheephead, rockfish and lingcod not found elsewhere.
  • Mediterranean: lower diversity with many endemics; Atlantic species enter via Gibraltar.

Saltwater fish identification FAQ

How do I tell a king mackerel from a Spanish mackerel?

Look at the lateral line and the first dorsal fin. On a king mackerel the lateral line drops sharply below the second dorsal, and the first dorsal is uniformly grey; a Spanish mackerel has a gently sloping lateral line, golden spots and a black patch on the front of the first dorsal.

Permit or pompano?

Permit are deeper-bodied and grow much larger; their dorsal and anal fin lobes are long and sickle-shaped and the belly often shows orange. Pompano stay under about 60 cm, have shorter fin lobes and a yellow belly and fins.

Why does my fish look different from the picture?

Saltwater fish change colour with depth, stress and death: a mahi-mahi fades from gold-green to grey within minutes on deck. Identify by shape, fin count, tooth pattern and lateral-line markings first, then use colour.
