Butterfly Fish Identification Chart: 13 Species with Pictures

There are more than 120 species in the family, and most of them are a variation on the same theme: a yellow-and-white disc with a black bar through the eye. This butterfly fish identification chart pairs a photo of each species divers and aquarists meet most with the one or two marks that make it unique: the eye-spot, the saddle, the banner, the snout. The family key below separates them from angelfish, and the care notes say which species survive in a tank.

How to identify a butterfly fish

  • Disc-shaped body. Strongly compressed sideways; nearly circular in profile, with a single continuous dorsal fin.
  • Eye bar. Almost every species runs a dark vertical bar through the eye to hide it.
  • Eye-spot (ocellus). Many carry a false eye near the tail or on the dorsal fin.
  • Snout length. Short-snouted species graze polyps and algae; long-snouted species (copperband, longnose) pick invertebrates from crevices.
  • No cheek spine. The single reliable difference from angelfish, which carry a spine on the preopercle. They also lack the angelfish's scale sheath at the base of the pelvic fins.
  • Pairs. Most species live in monogamous pairs and are seen two at a time.

Butterfly fish identification FAQ

How do I tell one from an angelfish?

Check the cheek: angelfish have a strong backward-pointing spine on the preopercle (the bone in front of the gill cover); butterflyfish do not. They are smaller, thinner and more disc-shaped, usually with a black bar through the eye.

Why do so many species have an eye-spot near the tail?

The false eye-spot, combined with a black bar hiding the real eye, makes predators strike the wrong end. Foureye, teardrop and copperband butterflyfish all use it; it is also a useful ID feature.

Are they reef-safe in an aquarium?

Mostly no. Many Chaetodon species are obligate coral feeders and starve in captivity. Raccoon, Klein's, Pakistani and vagabond accept frozen mysis and brine shrimp and are the species to choose; copperbands are demanding; saddleback and most obligate coral feeders should be left on the reef. Give any of them at least 55 gallons (200 L) and pristine water.
