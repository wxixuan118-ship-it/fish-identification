Salmon Identification Chart: 7 Species with Pictures

Seven salmon and steelhead share the same body plan, and in the ocean they are all silver. This salmon identification chart pairs a photo of each species with the features that hold up in hand: gum colour, where the spots sit, tail shape, anal-fin ray count and the colour each species turns at spawning. Anal fin rays are the most reliable check on a fish in hand: Pacific salmon have 13 or more, Atlantic salmon and steelhead 12 or fewer. The adipose fin, the small fleshy fin between the dorsal and the tail, is present on all of them and separates salmon from shad, herring and most other silver fish.

Salmon identification: Atlantic vs Pacific

Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) is the only salmon in the genus Salmo. It can survive spawning and return to the sea several times, which is why a bright fish in a river may carry healed scars. It is the salmon farmed worldwide, so a fish sold as "salmon" with no species name is almost always Atlantic.

Pacific salmon (genus Oncorhynchus) all die after their first spawn. Chinook and Coho are the two anglers most often confuse; Sockeye and Chum are the two with no spots; Pink is the smallest and runs on a two-year cycle. Steelhead belongs to the same genus and, like Atlantic salmon, can spawn more than once.

Salmon identification FAQ

How do I tell a Chinook from a Coho?

Open the mouth. A Chinook has a black gum line at the base of its teeth; a Coho has white gums (only the tongue is dark). Chinook also carry spots on both tail lobes, Coho on the upper lobe only.

Which salmon has no spots?

Sockeye and chum. Sockeye are slender with a large eye and turn red with a green head at spawning; chum show a faint grid pattern and white-tipped anal and pelvic fins, and develop purple tiger stripes.

Is steelhead a salmon?

Biologically it is a sea-run rainbow trout, but it is managed and fished like a salmon and is usually included on salmon identification charts. Unlike Pacific salmon it can survive spawning and return to the sea.
