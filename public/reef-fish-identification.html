Reef Fish Identification Chart: 24 Species with Pictures

Coral reefs hold about a quarter of all marine fish species, and most of them are small, colourful and fast. This reef fish identification chart puts a photo of each common species next to the traits that hold up underwater: silhouette, fin shape, bars and masks, and the scalpel spines, cheek spines and cleaning behaviour that separate look-alike families. Reef fish identification by family

Damselfish and clownfishPomacentridae

Small (5–15 cm), territorial, often vivid. Sergeant majors show bars, chromis school over coral, clownfish live in anemones.

WrassesLabridae

The most diverse reef family. Swim with the pectoral fins; terminal-phase males look nothing like females; cleaner wrasse run cleaning stations.

Surgeonfish and tangsAcanthuridae

Oval body with a sharp scalpel spine at the tail base. Blue, yellow and powder blue tangs are the iconic species.

AngelfishPomacanthidae

Like butterflyfish but with a prominent cheek spine. Juveniles often carry a completely different pattern from adults.

ButterflyfishChaetodontidae

Thin, disc-shaped, pointed snout; many carry an eye-mask or false eye-spot. Most live in pairs and pick at coral polyps.

Triggerfish and puffersBalistidae / Tetraodontidae

Triggerfish lock their first dorsal spine upright and can bite; puffers inflate and carry beak-like teeth.

Reef fish identification at depth

  • Red disappears first. Below about 10 m a red fish looks grey or brown. Carry a light or shoot with a strobe to restore true colour.
  • Yellow and blue survive longest. Tangs, angelfish and chromis are the easiest families to name without a light.
  • Silhouette before colour. Disc = butterflyfish or angelfish; oval with a tail-base spine = tang; angular with a locking dorsal = triggerfish; cigar-shaped swimming with pectoral fins = wrasse.
  • Behaviour is a trait. Cleaning stations mean cleaner wrasse; hovering schools over branching coral mean chromis; a fish defending a patch of algae is a damselfish.

Reef fish identification FAQ

Angelfish or butterflyfish?

Check the cheek. Angelfish carry a strong backward-pointing spine on the preopercle (the bone in front of the gill cover); butterflyfish do not. Butterflyfish are also smaller and thinner-bodied with a more pointed snout.

Why do reef fish look grey or brown at depth?

Water absorbs red light first, so below about 10 metres red and orange fish look grey or brown. Identify by silhouette and fin shape at depth and confirm colour with a dive light or a photo taken with a strobe.

Are juvenile reef fish on this chart?

Only where the juvenile is the form most people meet. Many reef species change completely with age — a blue tang starts yellow and an emperor angelfish juvenile wears white rings — so a juvenile that matches nothing is worth uploading to the identifier.
