Parrot Fish Identification Chart: 12 Species with Pictures

These beaked, technicolour grazers are found on every tropical reef, and they are also the reef fish most likely to be misnamed, because one species can wear two completely different colour phases. This parrot fish identification chart pairs a photo of each common species with the features that separate it: head shape, the pattern on the cheek and the tail-base spot. The family key below tells you how to recognise the family in the first place, and the AI identifier settles the rest.

How to identify a parrot fish

  • Beak-like teeth. Fused dental plates that look like a parrot's beak: unique among reef fish. You can hear them scraping coral underwater.
  • Large scales and a blunt head. Robust oval body, big cycloid scales, and in many species a steep or humped forehead.
  • Two colour phases. Initial phase fish are red, brown or striped; terminal-phase males are green and blue with pink and orange face markings. Juveniles can be a third pattern.
  • Pectoral-fin swimming. Like wrasses they row with the pectoral fins and use the tail only in bursts.
  • Mucus cocoon at night. Many species sleep in a transparent bubble of mucus wedged in the reef, a behavioural clue divers see on night dives.

Parrot fish habitat and behaviour

They live on coral reefs, seagrass beds and rocky shallows throughout the tropics, with the highest diversity in the Indo-Pacific and about 15 species in the Caribbean. They are diurnal grazers that scrape algae and dead coral with the beak, grind it with pharyngeal teeth and excrete the coral as fine white sand; a single large fish can produce up to 90 kg of sand a year. Most are protogynous hermaphrodites: fish start life as females in the initial phase and the largest change sex into terminal-phase males that hold a territory and a harem. Bumphead parrotfish are the exception, moving in herds of dozens and ramming coral heads to break them.

Parrot fish identification FAQ

How do I know it is not a wrasse?

Look at the mouth: teeth fused into a solid beak, where wrasses have separate, often protruding teeth. They are also stockier with large scales and a blunt head, and they swim with the same pectoral-fin stroke as wrasses.

Why do fish of the same species look so different?

Most species have an initial phase (usually drab red-brown or striped, mostly females) and a terminal phase (bright green-blue males). One fish can pass through both; the chart lists both forms where they are commonly seen.

Can you eat them?

They are eaten in many regions but are increasingly protected, because their grazing keeps algae off coral. Large species like bumphead and rainbow parrotfish are threatened. Check local rules; some islands ban the take of the whole family.
